## A robot's interface does not live on one screen

People read many cues at once when interacting with a robot. Does a sound signal confirmation? Does a movement indicate that the system is preparing to respond? Is the expression consistent with what is happening? Any one of these cues can change a person's judgment of the machine's state, intent, and reliability.

That makes human–robot interaction different from a conventional screen study. The interface is distributed across sound, movement, expression, distance, and timing. The user is not simply completing a task; they are continuously inferring what the machine will do next.

## My role

As a senior user researcher in 2019, I used experimental and field research to study how prompts, motion, and expression affected people's perception of service robots, their attitudes, and interaction efficiency. I also helped establish reusable research methods and documentation so that framing, execution records, and conclusions were more consistent across projects.

## One interaction contains four stages

I separated a complete service-robot encounter into four parts:

1. **Attraction** — does somebody notice the robot and choose to approach?
2. **Initialization** — do they understand what it can do and how to begin?
3. **Purposeful interaction** — does the system solve the problem through reasonable turns, timing, and behavior?
4. **Ending** — was the need resolved, why did the encounter stop, and would the person use it again?

Each stage fails differently. Task completion alone misses encounters that never begin, users who cannot tell what the machine is waiting for, and interactions with no clear way to finish. The stage model places sound, motion, expression, and state feedback back inside the job each cue actually performs.

## Answering causal and contextual questions

An experiment can control an interaction cue and compare what changes when that cue changes. A robot, however, eventually works in physical space. Other people may be talking. A user may not face the device directly. A gesture can be occluded, and waiting time can alter how intent is interpreted.

The work therefore needed two evidence lines:

- **Controlled comparison**, to examine whether a change in sound, movement, or expression corresponded with a difference in response.
- **Observation in context**, to reveal misunderstandings, detours, waiting, repeated commands, and recovery strategies that the experimental task did not anticipate.

Neither replaces the other. Experiments alone risk optimizing a cue outside the situation where it will be used. Field observation alone makes it difficult to isolate which cue contributed to a response.

When a capability was not yet built, I also used Wizard-of-Oz studies. A researcher simulated system behavior behind the scenes so that people could encounter an apparently working interaction. We could test whether they understood, accepted, and used the idea before committing engineering effort. The method is not a way to deceive people into validating a concept; it is a low-cost way to expose product assumptions, with clear consent and debriefing requirements.

## Turning “human-like” into a researchable question

Robot teams easily reach for words such as natural, friendly, or intelligent. Research has to rewrite those labels as observable behavior and comparable conditions. Did the person notice the feedback? Did they understand the current state? Did they know what to do next? Did they repeat the instruction? Were they willing to continue the interaction after completing the task?

This translation matters more than selecting a rating scale. It turns an aesthetic debate into hypotheses a product team can test. It also gives sound, motion, visual expression, and interaction logic a shared task around which to coordinate.

## What stayed

Beyond its product findings, the project strengthened research operations: when to use each method, how to record variables, how to preserve unexpected field events, and how to keep observation separate from interpretation in a conclusion. For rapidly changing AI hardware, that infrastructure outlasts any single result.

## A judgment I still hold

A robot has to make its intent readable while stating its limits plainly. Once people expect it to behave like a person, disappointment is almost inevitable. As systems act more and more like agents, the researcher's job expands beyond efficiency: it includes checking how people understand the system's intent, boundaries, and responsibility.
