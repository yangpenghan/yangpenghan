User research often proves its effort by counting problems. A larger number and a thicker report appear to signal greater value.

An issue list, however, is tightly coupled to the current version. Move a control, redesign a flow, or change a business rule and many findings lose their object. A team can fix this release while rebuilding the same behavioral difficulty through a different interface in the next one.

In 2018, a finance-app study ran for 24 days across six cities and 7,488 kilometres, completing 60 tests and interviews. The 3,600 minutes, 254GB of records, and roughly 600 pages became 86 experience issues. If the work had stopped at “we collected a lot of data,” the project would not yet have produced value. The difficult part was connecting each issue to a task, observed behavior, evidence segment, participant explanation, and priority.

## Protect the observation before explaining it

The greatest risk in moving from issues to patterns is telling a story too early.

“Users do not trust the product,” “users fear risk,” or “users do not understand finance” may all sound plausible. They can also absorb almost any evidence and become impossible to disprove. A more stable starting point is an observation another analyst could inspect: who was doing which task, what interface state was visible, what action occurred, and what followed.

I now force a distinction between three statements:

1. **Observation** — the participant returned to the previous screen twice and reread the fee information before confirming.
2. **Interpretation** — repeated checking may have been a strategy for restoring a sense of control.
3. **Decision** — keep consequential information visible at confirmation and test whether backtracking decreases.

The first statement can be supported by a record. The second is a model that needs competing explanations. The third is a product hypothesis that needs another behavioral test. Combining all three makes a recommendation sound certain while weakening its evidence.

## An explanation should pass four gates

Two similar stories are not enough to name a “type of user.” A useful behavioral explanation should pass at least four gates.

**Recurrence:** does it appear across participants, tasks, or locations? A single anomaly may be a lead, but not yet a stable explanation.

**Mechanism consistency:** can different surface actions plausibly arise from the same information-processing or decision difficulty? The reverse matters too. Identical pauses may represent reading, doubt, or waiting for system feedback and should not be merged automatically.

**Predictive value:** does the explanation help a team anticipate where another interface may fail? A category that only retells the past has little transfer value.

**Intervention value:** can design, process, or information change it? “The user lacks expertise” is not an actionable model. “Consequential information disappears before the decision, forcing reliance on memory” may be.

## A category is not a label for a person

Issue categories are easily turned into personality types: cautious users, impulsive users, low-cognition users. That moves a system-created burden onto the person and encourages a team to educate the user—or find a different user—instead of changing the conditions.

I am more interested in the relationship between a person and a situation. The same person may move confidently through a low-stakes task and repeatedly check an action involving money, privacy, or irreversible consequences. Personality did not suddenly change. The combination of consequence, information, and control did.

A useful explanation therefore describes an observable mechanism: information disappears before a consequential choice, system feedback conflicts with expectation, or the recovery route is unclear. Its purpose is not to classify people. It is to make the conditions created by the product visible.

## Lists and structures need each other

None of this makes an issue list obsolete. Product teams need the page, state, and task where a problem occurred in order to fix the current release. A structure also has to lead back to events; without traceable examples it is an untestable theory. The project ultimately organized 86 issues into navigation, feedback, visual design, consistency, terminology, and software defects, giving different owners a shared way to prioritize rather than a stream of isolated symptoms.

The strongest output has three layers:

- traceable behavioral events at the bottom;
- issue structures and competing explanations in the middle;
- product decisions with owners and validation plans at the top.

The list enables action. The structure enables learning. The next validation enables correction. Connected together, research becomes more than a health check for one release; it becomes the way a product gradually learns about people.

## A warning for AI-assisted analysis

Large models are good at naming themes quickly, which also makes them good at producing language that resembles a pattern. Automated clustering may help organize evidence, but it does not replace checks for recurrence, competing explanations, and intervention value.

If an AI-generated theme cannot point to the source events that support it, show evidence that conflicts with it, or propose a falsifiable next step, it is only a faster summary.

Issue lists expire with product versions. Good mechanism explanations age more slowly because they describe how attention, memory, trust, and control change under a class of conditions. But an explanation is not permanent truth either. New behavior must always be allowed to challenge it.
