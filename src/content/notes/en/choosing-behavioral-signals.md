One of the most persistent misunderstandings in behavioral research is treating an instrument list as a method. A proposal containing eye tracking, EEG, facial expression, physiology, and behavioral coding appears more advanced than one built around direct observation.

In practice, every additional signal creates another synchronization problem, quality-control process, analytical assumption, and interpretation risk. If it cannot change a decision, more data makes the study slower and gives the team more charts from which to select a preferred story.

In one dairy-packaging study, I used EEG, eye tracking, and natural behavior together. The useful idea was not the label “three modalities.” It was assigning each source a bounded responsibility—early response, visual path, and real action—and allowing them to meet only at shared task moments.

## Work backward from the decision

I usually ask four questions before discussing equipment.

First, **who needs to make which decision?** Is the team comparing concepts, reducing a critical use error, changing information hierarchy, or validating an automated workflow? Without a decision, there is no way to judge whether a signal is useful.

Second, **what observation would change that decision?** If the team will choose the same concept regardless of the result, research is decorating a choice already made.

Third, **can simpler evidence answer the question?** A problem visible through direct observation does not automatically need a physiological sensor. Language comprehension that an interview can explain should not be disguised as an EEG question.

Fourth, **how will we know the measurement failed?** Poor signal quality, clock drift, unsuitable samples, and overextended metrics need stopping rules before collection begins.

## Bounded responsibilities for five common sources

**Behavioral coding** describes what happened: action sequence, errors, near misses, recovery, waiting, and help-seeking. It is close to the task, but the coding rules and agreement between observers must be explicit.

**Eye tracking** describes allocation of visual attention: fixation, scanning, omission, and return. It does not directly establish comprehension, preference, or cognitive load. A long fixation may represent interest or difficulty.

**EEG** can compare task-related neural responses under a carefully controlled design. It is sensitive to artifacts, referencing, preprocessing, and analytical choice. A single metric cannot be translated directly into a complex mental state.

**Physiological signals** such as heart rate, skin conductance, and respiration can describe arousal or change. Alone, they rarely determine whether that change arose from anxiety, excitement, movement, or the environment.

**Facial expression** can help locate visible response patterns in time. It does not read a person's true emotion, and it cannot produce a certain interpretation outside cultural, contextual, and individual variation.

These boundaries are what make multi-source evidence meaningful in the first place: precisely because each signal is limited, other evidence is needed to cover what it cannot see.

## Multimodality is not a vote

A common move is to declare a conclusion supported when two of three sources—say gaze, survey, and physiology—“agree.” The signals do not measure the same construct and cannot be counted as a simple majority.

I prefer to organize them as a chain. A behavioral event locates where something changed. Gaze or physiology may rule out some explanations. An interview exposes a person's strategy. Task performance shows whether the change affected the goal. Each source should close a different gap.

When signals conflict, averaging them is rarely the first answer. Conflict may be the most useful finding. A person says they understand but repeatedly backtracks. They look directly at the critical information and still act incorrectly. That forces the team to examine the missing connection between seeing, understanding, and acting.

## Alignment matters more than the chart

Multimodal data can be interpreted together only when it shares events. Drift between device clocks, task markers, video frames, and manual coding can combine unrelated changes into a convincing story.

The work should therefore start with an event dictionary and synchronization design. What constitutes task start, feedback, error, recovery, and completion? Which system owns authoritative time? How are interruption and retry recorded? Without this foundation, more sophisticated association can produce more precise misinformation.

## When not to measure

I tend to remove a signal when:

- its result cannot change a product or research decision;
- the project cannot support synchronization, cleaning, and quality control;
- the metric is valid only in a controlled setting but the question requires natural action;
- the team wants it to prove a psychological label it already believes;
- the sensitivity of the data exceeds the decision value it adds.

Removing a signal demands the same clarity from the researcher about the question, the evidence, and the boundary.

The most mature behavioral study plans I have seen often use few instruments. What they share is that every column of data has a clear responsibility, and every conclusion knows where it must stop.
