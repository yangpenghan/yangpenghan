I studied psychology at East China Normal University, then spent four years teaching mental health and mathematics rather than going directly into a research organization.

That period rarely appears in a project description, but it is the beginning of how I work. A classroom demonstrates every day that understanding an explanation does not mean somebody will use it in another context. Applying it to an exercise does not mean the same behavior will survive time pressure, emotion, or real consequences.

Between understanding and change, there is always engineering work.

## Teaching first showed me a feedback loop

A lesson can be beautifully organized during preparation. In the classroom, attention, previous experience, peer relationships, and current emotion reorganize it. What the teacher says is only one input. Learning also depends on what the student does, which feedback follows, how an error is interpreted, and whether they are willing to try again.

I gradually asked less often whether I had explained something clearly, and more often where another person's action changed. The first question centers the speaker. The second requires observation of the whole system.

## A user can know and still be unable to act

The same gap appears repeatedly in research. A participant accurately describes a function but cannot find its entry during a task. They say packaging information is clear, then open it in an unintended sequence. They know an alarm matters but fail to notice it in a multitasking environment.

This does not mean users are dishonest. A verbal answer and an action in context face different cues and costs. An interview is useful for understanding an explanation. A task exposes action. Longer field observation shows how habits form. The method has to match the scale at which the problem occurs.

## Move from individual explanation to environmental design

Psychological training can tempt us to look for an individual trait: ability, motivation, or attitude. In many projects, however, the environment is the part a team can actually change.

A medical-device use error may emerge from information hierarchy, similar controls, handover process, and time pressure together. Driver distraction may not be a deficit of self-control; the system may demand unnecessary visual attention at the wrong moment. Repeated commands to a robot may not indicate resistance to AI; sound, motion, and state feedback may fail to form a consistent interaction grammar.

Once the question shifts from “what is wrong with this person?” to “how do people act under these conditions?”, products and organizations have something for which they can take responsibility.

## Systematization should not erase human variation

I later began building methods, templates, knowledge bases, and agentic workflows. Systematization sounds like making work uniform, but it should not compress human variation into an average.

The evidence chain is what needs consistency: how a question is framed, an observation recorded, an interpretation challenged, and a decision validated. Users, tasks, environments, and consequences are where important difference must remain. Standardize the first so the team can see the second more reliably.

This is also how I understand solution engineering. A template is not an answer; it prevents a team from skipping consequential questions. A tool is not an expert; it makes repeatable steps more dependable. AI is not the owner of judgment; it can help maintain context, call tools, and preserve provenance.

## Four questions I now use

Whether the work is teaching, research, or system design, I return to four similar questions:

1. Who should act differently, in which situation?
2. What cues, friction, and consequences does the current environment provide?
3. What evidence can distinguish “understood” from “can actually do it”?
4. If change occurs, how will the system continue to provide feedback and correct itself?

A proposal that explains why an idea is right but cannot answer these questions has not yet entered reality.

What I carried from the classroom was not a set of teaching techniques. It was a lasting sensitivity to whether action actually happens. Measuring gaze, behavior, or physiology; building a human-factors process; and orchestrating AI agents are all continuations of the same question: how can understanding pass through the constraints of the real world and become sustained action?
